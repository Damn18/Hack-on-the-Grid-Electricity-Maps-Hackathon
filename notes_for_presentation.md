## Business model

We charge the customers the average SPOT price at which we bought and sold energy for / from that specific EV, plus a fixed price per kWh to cover for our expanses: mainly salaries and cost for imbalance (what we have to pay to the TSO when an EV does not show up and we can't fulfill our bid).\
Eg: an EV needs 10kWh to charge. During its connection time, we buy 10kWh at 5 DKK/kWh, then 10kWh at 10 DKK/kWh, and we sell 10kWh at 15 DKK/kWh. In total, we paid 10x5 + 10x10 - 10x15 = 0 DKK, so we got the energy "for free". We would still make them pay 1DKK / kWh of fixed price, so we get 10DKK. 

We could later think of incentivising the customers to be connected as much as possible by paying them a fee per hour. This might coincidate with the expansion of our product to another market, the one that tackles energy arbitrage. The EVs would be batteries that we declare as available for grid management to the TSO, who would remunerate us for that. 

## What we bring 

- EV owners often charge ther cars when they come back home, in the evening. That is the peak hours for the electricity use, and prices. We would redce their bill by charging the batteries in the night, when the price is low. 
- This also helps reduce the load on the ditribution grid for electricity: the final kilometers network is often overloaded at those hours. 
- By using the EV's flexibility, we can buy electricity when it is the cheapest (and the least carbon-intense) and sell it when the production is lower: for high prices, and in order to reduce production from high-emissions energy. 
- Spreading the consumption over time optimizes the production of energy from renewable sources, which are sometimes curtailed because of a low consumption.
